Part I

The Pentateuch

The five books of Moses, and the ground everything after them argues with. They run from creation to the edge of the promised land and then stop, with the nation camped outside it and the man who brought them there buried on the wrong side of the river.

Genesis · Exodus · Leviticus · Numbers · Deuteronomy

01

Genesis A world spoken into order, and then a promise made to one childless couple that the editors insist is for everybody.

Events creation to c. 1805 BC·Written c. 1440 BC·Moses writing the deep past from the far side of the Exodus

Genre
Narrative · Torah
Author
Moses
Chapters
50
Setting
Mesopotamia, Canaan, Egypt
Theme
Beginnings, and a promise

Genesis opens with God speaking a world into order out of formless darkness and closes with a coffin in Egypt. Between those two points it tells its story twice over: first in eleven chapters of primeval history — creation, the garden and the fruit, Cain's murder of Abel, a flood that unmakes and remakes the world, and the tower at Babel where human ambition is scattered into nations — and then in thirty-nine chapters that narrow from all of humanity down to a single family. God calls Abram out of Ur with a promise of land, offspring and blessing for every nation on earth, and the rest of the book is that promise surviving everything that should have killed it: Sarah's barrenness, Abraham's near-sacrifice of Isaac, Jacob's theft of his brother's blessing and the twenty years of exile it costs him, and finally Joseph, sold into slavery by his own brothers, rising to run Egypt's grain supply and saving the family that betrayed him. The book ends with the promise intact but unfulfilled — seventy people living as guests in Egypt, a long way from a land of their own.

The first week, read across

The seam worth knowing about

Genesis divides itself. Eleven times it says "these are the generations of…" — the Hebrew toledot — and each one hands the story down a narrower channel: from the heavens and the earth, to Adam, to Noah, to Shem, to Terah, and on to Jacob's sons. The book is structured as a genealogy that keeps zooming in.

02

Exodus A slave nation walks out of the strongest empire on earth, and then spends the rest of the book learning to live with the God who got them out.

Exodus

Events c. 1876 – 1445 BC·Written c. 1440 BC·Four centuries of silence, then two years that change everything

Genre
Narrative · Torah
Author
Moses
Chapters
40
Setting
Egypt, the Red Sea, Mount Sinai
Theme
Rescue, and a God who moves in

Exodus opens four centuries after Genesis closes, with Jacob's seventy descendants grown into a population large enough to frighten a Pharaoh who does not remember Joseph. The state's answer is forced labour and then infanticide, and the book's answer to that is a baby floated down the Nile in a basket and raised in the household of the man trying to kill him. Moses grows up, kills an Egyptian, runs to Midian, and forty years later meets God in a bush that burns without being consumed and is sent back with a demand: let my people go. What follows is a contest between two claims to power, worked out through ten plagues that dismantle Egypt's gods one at a time and settled on the night of the Passover, when the nation walks out with the wealth of its captors and crosses a sea that opens ahead of them and closes on the army behind. That is the first half. The second half is stranger and slower. Three months out, the people are camped at Sinai, and God comes down on the mountain in fire and smoke to make a covenant with them — the ten commandments, then the law, and then thirteen chapters specifying the construction of a tent. They break the covenant almost immediately with a golden calf while Moses is still up the mountain collecting the terms, he smashes the tablets at the sight of it, and the rest of the book is the repair. It ends with no battle and no border, just a cloud filling the finished tabernacle: the God who was on the mountain now living in the middle of the camp.

The tent, from the outside in

Why the tent gets thirteen chapters

Modern readers tend to skim Exodus 25–40 and wonder why the instructions are given twice — once as a command, once as a report that it was done exactly so. The repetition is the point. The nation has just proved it cannot be trusted with a covenant, and the second telling is the text insisting, item by item, that this time they did what they were told.

03

Leviticus Twenty-seven chapters of instruction, folded in half, with the Day of Atonement at the exact centre.

Leviticus

Events about one month, 1445 BC·Written c. 1440 BC·The camp has not moved since Exodus ended — the shortest bar on the spine

Genre
Law · Torah
Author
Moses
Chapters
27
Setting
The foot of Mount Sinai
Theme
How to live next door to a holy God

Almost nothing happens in Leviticus. Nobody travels; the whole book takes place at the foot of the same mountain across roughly a month, and it is mostly God speaking to Moses out of the tent that was finished on the last page of Exodus. The question it answers is the one Exodus leaves hanging — if God is now living in the middle of the camp, how does the camp survive it? The answer arrives in movements: a manual of five offerings and who eats which parts, the ordination of Aaron and his sons, and a long section on what makes a person or a garment or a house unclean and what is done about it. The narrative stops twice, both times abruptly. Once for Nadab and Abihu, Aaron's own sons, who offer unauthorised fire and are killed where they stand while their father is told not to mourn. Once for a blasphemer, who is stoned. At the centre of the book sits chapter 16, the Day of Atonement, when the high priest goes behind the curtain once a year with blood and a second goat carries the nation's sin out into the wilderness. Everything after that turns outward, from ritual to ordinary conduct: wages paid the same day, the deaf not cursed, no stumbling block in front of the blind, the immigrant loved as yourself. It closes with a calendar of feasts, a sabbath year for the land itself, and a jubilee that cancels debt and hands property back every fiftieth year.

The book folded in half

The verse everyone quotes without the address

“Love your neighbour as yourself” is Leviticus 19:18, sitting in the middle of a chapter about leaving the edges of your field unharvested, not lying, not defrauding a hired worker overnight, and not slandering. Sixteen verses later the same chapter extends it: the foreigner living among you is to be loved on the same terms, “for you were foreigners in Egypt.”

04

Numbers Two censuses, thirty-eight years apart, with an entire generation missing in between.

Numbers

Events 1445 – 1406 BC·Written c. 1406 BC·Sinai to the plains of Moab, taking the long way round

Genre
Narrative · law
Author
Moses
Chapters
36
Setting
The wildernesses of Sinai, Paran and Zin
Theme
A generation that would not go in

Numbers takes its English name from the two censuses that bracket it, and the gap between those two figures is the story. The book opens with the nation organised to an almost military standard — counted, arranged by tribe around the tabernacle, the camp given a marching order — and sets off from Sinai toward the land they were promised, a journey of about eleven days. They reach the border, send twelve scouts in, and ten come back reporting fortified cities and giants. The nation believes the ten, refuses to go up, and gets as far as proposing they elect a leader to take them back to Egypt. The sentence for that refusal is forty years in the wilderness, one year for each day the scouts were gone, until everyone of fighting age at the border has died — and most of the book is that sentence being served. Korah leads a rebellion and the ground opens under it. Moses, out of patience after four decades of complaint, strikes a rock instead of speaking to it and is told he will not enter either. Snakes come, and a bronze serpent is raised on a pole. A foreign prophet named Balaam is hired to curse Israel, is out-argued by his own donkey, and blesses them four times against his employer's explicit and increasingly expensive instructions. Then the second census closes the book: 601,730 fighting men, against the 603,550 counted at the start — nearly the same number, almost none of them the same people. Of the generation that stood at the border, only Joshua and Caleb are still standing.

Eleven days, and then thirty-eight years

The two numbers are the argument

The censuses come out almost identical, which is the point of putting them at either end. God's promise about the size of the nation survives intact; what does not survive is the particular generation that heard it. The book is not about attrition but about substitution — the promise keeps, the people are replaced.

05

Deuteronomy Moses' last three speeches, laid out in the exact form of a treaty between a great king and a subject people.

Deuteronomy

Events about one month, 1406 BC·Written c. 1406 BC·East of the Jordan, in sight of a land the speaker will not enter

Genre
Law · covenant renewal
Author
Moses
Chapters
34
Setting
The plains of Moab
Theme
Choose life

Deuteronomy is Moses talking. The nation is camped east of the Jordan, the forty years are over, and the man who brought them out is not going in — so he spends his last month giving three long speeches to a generation that was too young to remember Egypt. The first retells the journey, including the refusal at the border and his own disqualification, so that people who did not live the history own it anyway. The second restates the law: the ten commandments a second time, then the Shema — hear, O Israel, the LORD our God, the LORD is one — and then chapter after chapter of specifics on courts, kings, warfare, gleaning, runaway slaves, widows and immigrants. The third lays out terms: blessings if they keep the covenant, curses if they do not, spelled out at a length that is genuinely hard to read and impossible to misunderstand. The whole document is shaped like a treaty of a kind the surrounding empires actually used, and it turns on one repeated demand that no treaty ever made — love the LORD your God with all your heart. Moses appoints Joshua, teaches the nation a song so they will not be able to forget the terms, blesses the tribes, and climbs Mount Nebo to look across at the land. He dies there, and the book says nobody knows where he is buried.

Read against the form it borrows

Where the name comes from, and why it misleads

“Deuteronomy” is Greek for second law, from a mistranslation in 17:18 of a phrase that actually means a copy of this law. It is not a second, different law. It is the same covenant restated for people who were children when it was first given — which is why it repeats so much and explains so much more than Exodus does.

06

Joshua The land taken in twelve chapters and divided in twelve more — and the dividing is the half the book cares about.

Joshua

Events c. 1406 – 1375 BC·Written c. 1375 BC·One generation, immediately after Deuteronomy ends

Genre
Narrative · history
Author
Joshua
Chapters
24
Setting
Canaan, west of the Jordan
Theme
The promise, finally in hand

Joshua opens the morning after Deuteronomy closes, with a new commander being told three times to be strong and courageous, which suggests he was neither. The river is in flood; the priests carry the ark into it and it stops. Jericho falls to a march and a shout rather than a siege, and the first thing that goes wrong is not military but internal — a man named Achan keeps plunder he was told to destroy, and the next assault fails until it is dealt with. After that the campaigns move quickly: a coalition of southern kings defeated in a single long day, a northern coalition broken at the waters of Merom, and a summary list of thirty-one defeated kings that reads like an inventory. Then the book changes gear entirely. Twelve chapters of borders, towns and tribal allotments follow, dense with place names, and the tedium is the point: this is the deed of title, the promise made to Abraham written out in surveyors’ language. It ends with Joshua old, gathering the nation at Shechem, reciting their history back to them and asking them to choose whom they will serve — and warning them, flatly, that he does not think they are capable of it.

07

Judges The same five steps, six times over, and the nation is worse at the end of each round than it was at the start.

Judges

Events c. 1375 – 1050 BC·Written c. 1043 BC·Three centuries with no king and no capital

Genre
Narrative · history
Author
Samuel, by tradition
Chapters
21
Setting
Canaan, tribe by tribe
Theme
Everyone did what was right in their own eyes

Judges covers roughly three centuries in which there is no king, no capital and very little unity — the tribes act separately, and the book is a set of regional stories rather than a national history. It runs on a cycle stated plainly in chapter 2 and then repeated: the people abandon the covenant, God hands them to a neighbouring power, they are oppressed until they cry out, God raises up a deliverer, and the land has rest until that deliverer dies, at which point it begins again. The deliverers get less impressive each time. Othniel is exemplary and gets six verses. Deborah has to lead because Barak will not go without her. Gideon needs three separate signs before he will act and ends up making an object the nation worships. Jephthah wins a battle and loses his daughter to a vow he did not have to make. Samson, who gets four chapters, breaks every term of his own consecration and accomplishes most of what he accomplishes out of personal grievance. The book then closes with two appendices — a stolen shrine and a civil war that begins with a gang rape and nearly wipes out a tribe — and repeats its refrain: in those days there was no king in Israel, and everyone did what was right in his own eyes.

Ruth Four chapters set during the worst period in the book before it, and almost nothing bad happens in them.

Ruth

Genre

Chapters

Setting

Loyalty, and a genealogy that matters later

Ruth is set in the same period as Judges and is its deliberate opposite: no armies, no atrocities, four chapters in which ordinary people behave decently. A famine drives an Israelite family into Moab, where the father and both sons die, leaving three widows. Naomi tells her Moabite daughters-in-law to go home; one does, and Ruth refuses in the book’s best-known lines, binding herself to a woman with nothing to offer and to a God not her own. They return to Bethlehem at barley harvest, destitute, and Ruth goes out to glean — a provision written into Leviticus, the corners of the field left unharvested for the poor and the immigrant, here shown working exactly as intended. She gleans in the field of Boaz, a relative, who notices her, protects her and feeds her. Naomi then engineers a proposal, Boaz navigates a nearer kinsman with a legal manoeuvre at the town gate, and they marry. The child is handed to Naomi, and the women of the town tell her that her daughter-in-law, a foreigner, is worth more to her than seven sons. Then the book does the thing it has been building to all along and gives four generations of genealogy, ending on a name nobody in the story could have known was coming.

1 Samuel Israel asks for a king so it can be like the other nations, and gets exactly what it asked for.

1 Samuel

Events c. 1105 – 1010 BC·Written c. 1000 BC·The last judge, the first king, and the next one waiting

Genre

Chapters

Theme
Israel asks for a king

First Samuel turns on a request. The priesthood at Shiloh is corrupt, the ark is captured by the Philistines and returned because it is more trouble than it is worth, and Samuel — prophet, judge, and the last of a line that ran back to Moses — grows old with sons who take bribes. So the elders ask for a king, to judge them like all the nations. Samuel takes it as a personal rejection; God tells him it is not personal, and instructs him to warn them exactly what a king will cost: your sons for his army, your daughters for his kitchens, a tenth of everything, and no way back. They ask anyway. Saul is tall, handsome, initially reluctant and briefly impressive, and then unravels over a series of small disobediences that he keeps explaining rather than admitting. Samuel privately anoints a shepherd boy from Bethlehem, and the last two thirds of the book are the two men’s lives running in opposite directions — David rising through a giant, a friendship with Saul’s own son, and years as a fugitive commanding a company of the discontented and indebted, while Saul deteriorates into paranoia, massacres a town of priests, and finally consults a medium the night before dying on Mount Gilboa with his sons.

2 Samuel David’s reign, hinged in the middle on an afternoon he should have spent at war.

2 Samuel

Second Samuel is one reign, and it divides cleanly in half. In the first, everything works: David is made king over Judah and then over all Israel, takes the Jebusite fortress that becomes Jerusalem, brings the ark into the city dancing in front of it, beats every neighbouring army, and is given a promise through the prophet Nathan that his house and his kingdom will endure — the covenant the rest of the Old Testament keeps returning to. Then, in the spring, at the time when kings go out to war, David stays home. What follows is adultery, a pregnancy, a failed cover-up, and the arranged death of the husband, a man loyal enough that the plan only works because he refuses to sleep in his own house while the army is in the field. Nathan tells him a story about a stolen lamb, David convicts himself, and the sentence is that the sword will not depart from his house. It does not: his eldest son rapes his daughter, another son murders that son and then raises a rebellion that drives David barefoot out of his own city, and the king ends the book as an old man taking a census he was warned not to take.

1 Kings A temple, a golden age, and then the kingdom splits in two over a tax question.

1 Kings

First Kings opens with a succession fight at David’s deathbed and settles into Solomon, who asks for wisdom rather than wealth and is given both, builds the temple over seven years and his own palace over thirteen, and turns Jerusalem into the sort of place foreign monarchs travel to see. The account is admiring and then, quietly, is not: the same king accumulates horses from Egypt, silver, and seven hundred wives with political attachments, and by the end of his reign there are shrines to their gods on the hills outside the city, item by item against the specific warnings in Deuteronomy about what a king must not do. His son Rehoboam inherits the kingdom, is asked at his coronation to lighten the forced labour his father imposed, takes advice from his contemporaries instead of his father’s counsellors, and promises to make it heavier. Ten tribes walk out. From that point the book runs on two tracks at once, north and south, cutting between them reign by reign, and the northern track goes bad immediately: Jeroboam sets up two golden calves so his people will not travel to Jerusalem to worship, and every northern king after him is measured against that. The last third belongs to Elijah, Ahab and Jezebel, and the contest on Mount Carmel.

2 Kings Both kingdoms fall, a hundred and thirty-five years apart, and the book explains at length why neither was a surprise.

2 Kings

Genre

Chapters

Second Kings continues the two-track narrative and takes both tracks to their ends. It opens with Elijah going up in a whirlwind and Elisha inheriting a double portion of his spirit, and the Elisha cycle that follows is the most miracle-dense stretch in the Old Testament outside Exodus — a widow’s oil, a poisoned stew, a floating axe head, a Syrian general cured of leprosy after being told to wash in a river he considers beneath him. Then the political narrative closes in. Assyria takes tribute, then territory, and in 722 BC the northern kingdom ends: Samaria falls after a three-year siege, the population is deported and replaced, and the narrator stops the story to explain in a long, unusually direct passage that this happened because of everything the book has been documenting since Jeroboam. Judah survives, and produces two of its best kings in Hezekiah and Josiah — Josiah finds a lost scroll of the law during temple repairs, reads it aloud, tears his clothes and launches the most thorough reform in the book. It is not enough, and the narrator says so. Babylon comes in three waves, and in 586 BC the walls are breached, the temple burned, the last king blinded after watching his sons killed, and the population marched east. The book ends on a small strange note of hope: thirty-seven years later, an exiled Judean king is released from prison in Babylon and given a seat at the royal table.

David, and the temple he was not allowed to build

First Chronicles opens with nine chapters that are almost entirely names — Adam, Seth, Enosh, on through Noah and Abraham and Judah to David, then the tribes, then the priests, then the families who came back from Babylon. Modern readers skip it; those last families were the point of it. This is a history written for people who have just returned from exile to a small province with no king, a half-built temple and a reasonable suspicion that they have become a footnote. The genealogy is the answer to that suspicion: you are the same people, the line is unbroken, and it runs from the first man to the person holding this scroll. The narrative proper starts at chapter 10, disposes of Saul in a single paragraph, and gives everything after it to David — but a different David from the one in Samuel. There is no Bathsheba, no Absalom, no civil war, no deathbed score-settling. What there is instead is David organising: the ark brought up to Jerusalem with three chapters of Levites and singers, the threshing floor bought that will become the temple site, and then the plans, the materials, the courses of priests, the gatekeepers and the treasurers, all arranged in advance for a building he is told plainly he will not construct. It ends with him handing his son a finished blueprint and a national fundraising campaign.

2 Chronicles The same four centuries as Kings, with the northern kingdom almost entirely left out and a different question being asked.

2 Chronicles

Genre
Narrative · retelling
Author
Ezra, by tradition
Chapters

Setting

Theme
Measured by what they did about the temple

Second Chronicles covers the same four centuries as First and Second Kings and tells them very differently. Solomon gets nine chapters, almost all of it temple: the construction, the dedication, the prayer, the glory filling the house. Then the kingdom divides, and the northern ten tribes essentially disappear from the account — no Elijah cycle, no Elisha, no Ahab and Jezebel, no fall of Samaria except as background noise. The book follows the line of David alone, king by king, and measures each one against a single question: what did he do about the temple. Hezekiah, who gets three verses of reform in Kings, gets four chapters here, because he reopened the doors and kept the Passover. Josiah’s repairs and the scroll found during them get the same weight. And Manasseh, who in Kings is simply the worst king Judah ever had, is given something Kings never mentions: a deportation to Babylon in chains, a prayer of repentance, and a restoration — because this book is written for people who have themselves been deported and need to know whether coming back is possible. It ends not with the temple burning, which it reports in a handful of verses, but with Cyrus of Persia decreeing that the house in Jerusalem be rebuilt. The last sentence is an invitation: whoever is among you of all his people, let him go up.

Ezra Two returns sixty years apart, a temple finished under protest, and a scribe who is horrified by what he finds.

Genre
Narrative · history
Author
Ezra
Chapters

Setting
Babylon, and Jerusalem
Theme
The temple rebuilt, and a community redefined

Ezra picks up on the decree that ends Chronicles and follows two waves of return, sixty years apart. The first, under Zerubbabel in 538 BC, brings something like fifty thousand people back to a ruined city with imperial authorisation to rebuild the temple. They lay the foundation, and the account records the crowd doing two things at once: younger people shouting for joy, and older people who had seen the first temple weeping so loudly that nobody could tell the two sounds apart. Then the work stops. Local officials write to the Persian court, construction is halted by imperial order for about sixteen years, and it restarts only when the prophets Haggai and Zechariah begin preaching and a search of the archives at Ecbatana turns up Cyrus’s original decree, still on file. The temple is finished in 516. Job is set outside Israel, mentions no covenant, no law, no temple and no Jewish history, and is written in some of the hardest Hebrew in the Bible. It opens with a wager. A man of exemplary integrity loses his livestock, his servants, his ten children and finally his health inside two chapters — and the reader is told exactly why, through a challenge in the divine court about whether anyone is good for nothing, whether Job fears God for God or for the herds. Job himself is never told. Three friends arrive and sit with him on the ground for seven days without speaking, which is the wisest thing anyone does in the book. Then they open their mouths, and thirty-five chapters of increasingly heated poetry follow in three cycles: the friends argue with mounting confidence that suffering follows sin and that Job should confess, and Job insists he has nothing to confess and demands a hearing. He is not asking for comfort. He is asking for a trial. A fourth speaker, Elihu, appears from nowhere, is never properly introduced and is never answered afterwards. Then God arrives in a whirlwind, never mentions the wager, never explains, and never answers the question — instead asking about seventy of his own. Where were you when I laid the foundations. Do you know when the mountain goats give birth. Did you give the horse his strength. Look at the ostrich, who is spectacularly stupid and outruns you anyway. Job withdraws his case. And then the friends, who spent the whole book defending God, are told they have not spoken rightly of him, and that Job — who accused him to his face — has.

Psalms is not a narrative but a collection — 150 poems gathered across roughly a thousand years and arranged into five books, each closing with a doxology, in deliberate echo of the five books of Moses. The voices in it run the full range of what people say to God: victory songs and coronation hymns, tightly argued meditations on the law, liturgies for pilgrims climbing the road to Jerusalem, and a great deal of complaint. Something like a third of the collection is lament — psalms that accuse God of sleeping, of forgetting, of hiding his face — and whoever assembled the book left them in without softening them. The arc of the whole is visible if you read it straight through as a book rather than dipping in: it opens with the confident promise that the righteous prosper like a tree planted by water, spends its middle in the wreckage of a monarchy that failed and a temple that burned, and climbs out through the final book into five uninterrupted hymns of praise. The first nine chapters are not proverbs at all but a sustained address from a father to a son, and they are the key to everything that follows: wisdom is personified as a woman calling in the street, folly as a rival woman with a competing invitation, and the choice between them is presented as the choice that organises a life. Ecclesiastes is the strangest book in the Old Testament and the one most people are surprised to find in it. Its key word, hevel, appears nearly forty times and is usually translated “vanity” or “meaningless”, but it literally means vapour or breath — something real that you cannot hold. The Preacher applies it to everything in turn, and he does not argue the case abstractly; he runs experiments. He tries pleasure, wine, great works, houses, vineyards, pools, singers, silver and gold, and reports that he denied himself nothing his eyes desired. He tries wisdom, and finds that the wise man and the fool meet the same end and are forgotten equally. He tries work, and observes that everything he built will pass to a successor who may well be an idiot. He notices that the righteous perish and the wicked prosper, that the courts are corrupt, that the oppressed have no comforter, and that a living dog is better than a dead lion. What he keeps returning to, without ever resolving the tension, is a small recurring instruction: eat your bread, drink your wine, enjoy your work and the person you live with, because these are given and today is what you have. The book closes with an epilogue in a different voice, and the last word is not despair but limit — fear God and keep his commandments, because God will bring every deed into judgement, including every hidden thing.

He calls the intruders blemishes at the love feasts, shepherds feeding only themselves, waterless clouds, autumn trees without fruit and twice dead, wild waves foaming up their own shame, wandering stars for whom the deepest darkness has been reserved. It is the most concentrated invective in the New Testament. And then, having spent the whole letter condemning, he turns at the last moment to the congregation itself with something completely different in tone: build yourselves up, pray, keep yourselves in the love of God — and have mercy on those who doubt, snatch others out of the fire, and show mercy to still others mixed with fear. The closing doxology, to him who is able to keep you from stumbling, is read at the end of services to this day.

Quoting books that are not in the Bible

Verses 14–15 quote 1 Enoch by name as prophecy, and verse 9 — the archangel Michael disputing with the devil over the body of Moses — appears to come from the Assumption of Moses. Neither is in any Jewish or Protestant canon. It is the clearest case in the New Testament of a writer drawing on literature his readers evidently knew well, and it made Jude one of the last books to be accepted.

Revelation is a letter, a prophecy and an apocalypse at once, written by John from exile on Patmos to seven real congregations in western Asia Minor that were under real pressure to compromise with Rome. It opens with dictated messages to those seven — praise, rebuke and warning, church by church, each ending with a promise to whoever holds out — and then John is called up through an open door in heaven and shown the throne room, where a scroll sealed seven times can be opened by no one at all until a slaughtered Lamb takes it. What follows is three telescoping cycles of seven, in which the created order comes apart under judgment, interrupted twice by visions that reframe the whole conflict: a woman and a dragon, a beast out of the sea and the mark it puts on people, Babylon the great riding the beast in imperial luxury and falling in a single hour while the merchants of the earth stand at a distance and weep for their lost trade. The imagery is drawn almost entirely from Daniel, Ezekiel, Exodus and Isaiah, and it is doing to Rome exactly what those books did to Babylon. It ends with the dragon defeated, the dead judged out of the books, and the new Jerusalem coming down out of heaven — the garden of Genesis rebuilt as a city, with the tree of life growing in the middle of the street and no temple anywhere in it, because God is living there in person.

Read the footnotes

Revelation quotes or alludes to the Old Testament several hundred times and never once cites it directly. If the imagery feels arbitrary, that is usually a sign the source is Ezekiel or Daniel and the reference has gone past. It is the last book in the canon and the least readable without the first sixty-five.

On the dates, and the sources

This site follows the traditional chronology. That means Mosaic authorship of the Pentateuch, an Exodus around 1446 BC, a single eighth-century Isaiah, and a sixth-century Daniel. It is a coherent position and it is not the only one. Critical scholarship dates much of the Pentateuch to the monarchy and later, splits Isaiah into two or three authors, and places Daniel in the second century BC. Where a book's date is genuinely contested, the difference can be several hundred years, which on the spine above would move a dot most of the way across an era. Read the timeline as one defensible reconstruction rather than as settled fact.
